How to Read a COA

A Certificate of Analysis only protects you if you know how to read it. Here’s what every section means, what actually matters, and the red flags that should make you walk away.

Educational content only — not professional advice. This lesson teaches you how to interpret lab reports; it is not guidance on purchasing or using any product.
01

What Is a COA?

The report card for what’s in the vial.

A Certificate of Analysis is a laboratory report summarizing the tests run on a product. Think of it as a report card — instead of grading math or science, it grades the quality of the sample that was submitted.

A good COA can answer questions like:

🧪

Identity

Is this actually the peptide it claims to be?

⚖️

Quantity

How much peptide is in the vial?

💧

Purity

How pure is the sample?

🛡️

Safety

Was it tested for contaminants — sterility, heavy metals, or endotoxins?

Not every report covers every test — some are basic, some are comprehensive. And here’s the part most people miss: a COA is only as trustworthy as the lab that produced it and the sample that was tested.

02

Reading the Header

Before the numbers, check the paperwork.

Four details tell you whether the report even applies to what you’re holding.

🏷️

Product Name

The compound tested — whatever it may be. Obvious, but always confirm it matches what you’re actually buying.

🔢

Batch / Lot Number

One production run, one number — like a single recipe. If your vial’s batch doesn’t match the COA’s batch, your vial wasn’t necessarily tested.

📅

Testing Date

A report from years ago says nothing about today’s batches. Reliable suppliers retest as new batches are made — all-old COAs are worth asking about.

🔬

Lab Information

Name, contact, report number, analyst, issue date. Reports you can independently verify (QR codes, online lookup) beat random screenshots.

04

Two real reports, decoded

Where to look, and what to check, on actual COAs.

Two retatrutide reports, shared for education. The numbered markers show where each key detail lives — lab, report ID, batch, dates, results, verification — and the notes explain why each one matters.

Report A — Janoshik

  1. Janoshik — the lab. A report is only as good as its source; this is who ran the tests.
  2. Task #216 — the report’s ID number. You need it to verify the document.
  3. Sample: “Retatrutide 20mg.” Exactly what was submitted, as labeled — the client line names Ape Biotech Limited.
  4. Batch 2026 — the production batch (manufacturer listed simply as “ape”). It must match the vial in your hand.
  5. Dates in order — ordered Jul 30, received Jul 31, analyzed Aug 5, 2026. Recent, and the sequence makes sense.
  6. Blind test — the lab screened for three possible compounds — semaglutide, tirzepatide, and retatrutide — without being told which. Stronger identity evidence than a targeted test.
  7. 22.59 mg measured — identity confirmed as retatrutide, at about 13% over the 20 mg label. Overfill is common; the point is label vs. measured.
  8. Purity 99.535% — very clean. But purity ≠ quantity: this number alone doesn’t give you the 22.59 mg.
  9. Verification key IRCG44 + QR — look it up at janoshik.com/verify/. A checkable key beats a screenshot.
  10. Signature — the analyst’s sign-off. Accountability.

What’s missing: no sterility, endotoxin, or heavy-metals sections — identity, quantity, and purity only.

Report B — Accumark Labs

  1. Accumark Labs — the lab name, top of the header.
  2. Sample code JGAU-X699 — this report’s identifier.
  3. Received 10/01/2026 · Published 10/05/2026 — recent, four days apart.
  4. Lot 100 — the batch. Compare it against your vial.
  5. Results table — identity conforms; quantity 10.137 mg measured (about 1.4% over the 10.0 mg size); purity 99.963% against a ≥98% specification — conforms. The spec column states the acceptance bar up front.
  6. HPLC chromatogram — the raw purity evidence. One tall peak on a flat baseline means a clean separation.
  7. Two signatories — reviewed by a lab manager (PhD) and certified by a lab director (MD), both dated 10/05/2026. Dual accountability.
  8. QR code — a verification path; the lab’s homepage is printed, but no separate report-key URL appears on the page.

What’s missing: no sterility, endotoxin, or heavy-metals sections. No standalone report-verification link — only the lab’s homepage.

03

Identity Testing

The most important section on the report.

One question: is this actually the peptide it claims to be?

If identity fails, nothing else matters — a sample can show perfect purity and perfect sterility and still be the wrong compound entirely. Identity first, everything else second.

05

Assay (Content)

How much peptide is actually in there.

Assay tells you how much peptide is actually in the sample — whether the contents match the label. A vial labeled 10 mg that only contains 7 mg can still look pure; you’d just be getting less than you paid for.

Many experienced buyers rank assay among the most important sections, because it directly answers whether the product contains the expected amount.

Diagram · Same amount, different purity

Purity doesn’t tell you how much is in the vial — that’s assay’s job. Illustration, not measured data.

06

Purity

How clean is the sample — and why it confuses everyone.

Purity measures how clean the sample is — the share of desired peptide versus impurities and byproducts. This is where most confusion lives:

Purity does not tell you how much peptide is in the vial.

Picture a glass of nearly pure orange juice next to the same glass of juice mixed with water and pulp. Same amount of liquid — very different purity. That’s why purity and assay must always be read together, never alone.

07

Sterility Testing

Hunting for living microorganisms.

Sterility testing hunts for living microorganisms — bacteria or fungi that may have contaminated the product during manufacturing. A passing result means no microbial growth was detected in the tested sample.

One caveat: sterility describes the sample at the time it was tested. Careless handling after the vial reaches you can still introduce contamination.

Diagram · Two different questions

Sterility and endotoxin answer different questions — you need both answers.

08

Endotoxin Testing

Not sterility — bacterial toxins.

Endotoxin testing is not sterility testing. Instead of looking for living bacteria, it measures bacterial toxins that remain after the bacteria are dead.

This is one of the most misunderstood sections in peptide testing. Sterility and endotoxin answer different questions — both are useful, neither replaces the other.

09

Heavy Metals & Sign-Off

Contaminants and accountability.

⚗️

Heavy Metals

Looks for lead, mercury, cadmium, and arsenic — contaminants that can enter through raw materials, equipment, or the environment.

✍️

Signature & Approval

An analyst or reviewer signs off on the report, documenting who verified the results. It adds accountability and confirms testing was completed.

10

A COA Isn’t a Magic Shield

One data point, not a verdict.

The most common mistake: assuming that merely having a COA makes a supplier trustworthy. Run through these questions every time:

🏛️

Is the laboratory reputable?

A report is only as good as its source.

📅

Is the report recent?

Old reports don’t describe current batches.

🔢

Does the batch number match

the product in your hand?

📋

Are the results complete

— identity, assay, purity, and safety testing?

✅

Can the report be independently verified

, not just screenshotted?

🔄

Does the supplier test new batches consistently

, or rest on one old report?

Trustworthy suppliers combine good testing with transparency, consistency, and a track record.

11

Reputable Testing Labs

A report is only as good as its source.

A COA is only as trustworthy as the lab behind it. These six independent labs show up regularly on legitimate third-party peptide reports — each runs its own instruments, issues its own certificates, and offers a way to verify a report independently.

Janoshik

One of the most widely referenced independent labs in the peptide community. Reports typically pair HPLC purity with LC-MS identity confirmation, and each certificate carries a unique key you can check on their site.

MZ Biolabs

An analytical lab in Tucson, Arizona using HPLC with UV detection plus mass spectrometry. Their COAs typically report purity percentage, measured quantity per vial, and molecular-weight identity confirmation.

Kovera Labs

A US-based third-party lab running HPLC-MS on research peptides. Reports cover purity, net content, and LC-MS identity — often with endotoxin, heavy metal, and sterility screens — and every certificate is published to a public verifier.

Peptide Test Canada

A Canadian lab offering HPLC purity analysis plus LC-MS/MS identity confirmation, with a scannable QR code on every COA and optional heavy metals and endotoxin add-ons.

Freedom Diagnostics

A US-based analytical service used by many research peptide suppliers. Reports pair HPLC-UV purity with mass spectrometry identity, and each COA carries an accession code you can look up on their site.

PepScan Australia

An independent analytical lab in Sydney using HPLC and mass spectrometry for peptide identity, purity, and concentration testing — with fast turnaround and verifiable digital reports.

A familiar lab name is a good sign — but it’s not the finish line. Always verify the report itself through the lab’s own lookup, and make sure the batch number matches the vial in your hand.

12

Beginner Mistakes

The traps almost everyone falls into at first.

🎯

Purity tunnel vision

Staring only at the purity percentage while ignoring assay, identity, and date.

🔢

Ignoring the batch number

The report may not describe your vial at all.

📅

Skipping the issue date

Never checking the date on the report.

⚖️

Assuming equal standards

Assuming every lab follows the same standards. They don’t.

📄

Trusting the logo

Believing any PDF with a lab logo must be authentic. Verify independently.

🔀

Mixing up the tests

Mixing up purity, assay, sterility, and endotoxin — four different answers.

🕰️

One report forever

Treating one old COA as a lifetime guarantee for every future batch.

13

Key Takeaways

Pin this to memory.

  • A COA is a lab report — not a guarantee of quality by itself.
  • Confirm the product name matches what you’re buying.
  • Check the batch number whenever you can.
  • Check the testing date — recent matters.
  • Identity confirms what the product is.
  • Assay measures how much peptide is present.
  • Purity measures how clean the sample is.
  • Sterility and endotoxin answer different questions.
  • Heavy metals screening looks for specific contaminants.
  • A trustworthy supplier offers more than a nice-looking PDF.
